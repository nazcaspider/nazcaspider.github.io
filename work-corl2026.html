The tenth CoRL drew 2,094 submissions, about three times the roughly 700 of 2025, and around 4,800 attendees across three Austin venues. Program chairs kept the 32.8% acceptance rate and a single track by adding a poster-only tier and 3-minute spotlights. The top submission keywords were manipulation, imitation learning, perception/control representations, RL and robot foundation models, and the accepted papers followed that distribution closely. A new late-breaking track on agentic robotics and real-to-sim-to-real responded to frontier models released only months before.

On stage, the technical picture was more settled than the strategic one. Physical Intelligence, NVIDIA, Google DeepMind and many academic labs presented the same stack: a large BC-pretrained policy, human egocentric data for scale, world models for data and evaluation, and a frontier model on top for planning. The disagreement was about whether this stack reaches reliable, profitable deployment. Polls before and after the closing debate on that question leaned against the motion and barely moved. Ken Goldberg (UC Berkeley) described the field as being in a 'revenue crisis', meaning it now has to deliver reliability, throughput and interpretability.

Themes

Frontier models on top, fast policies underneath

Most systems put a slow reasoner over a fast learned controller. Hang Zhao (Tsinghua University / Galaxea AI) reported that frontier models used as step-by-step policies under a real clock fell to 15-20% success on RoboCasa, and argued that most failures happen at the handoffs between layers. Boston Dynamics said no off-the-shelf VLM was reliable enough to check task completion without fine-tuning. In the debate, Vincent Vanhoucke (Waymo) said general-purpose models were beating the conference's robot models on some robotics benchmarks; Pulkit Agrawal (MIT / Eka Robotics) answered that contact-rich System 1 data is still missing from those models.

See: Organizing Physical AI Across Timescales; From Dreaming to Doing: World Action Models and Agentic Robotics; Actions and Agents for True Long-Horizon Behavior.; Playful Agentic Robot Learning

World models used as policies, simulators and evaluators

DreamZero and GR00T 2 jointly denoise video and actions. DreamZero's authors found that failures originate mostly in video generation, so better video backbones should mean better actions. SuperMimic won Best Paper by training a humanoid tracker through one learned differentiable dynamics model in about three GPU-hours. Tesla warned that training inside a world model, rather than only evaluating with one, lets policies exploit its physical errors. Another spotlight showed world models can be poisoned.

See: World Action Models are Zero-shot Policies; SuperMimic: Scaling Humanoid Whole-Body Control with One Differentiable World Model; GRAIL: Generating Humanoid Loco-Manipulation from World Models; Building Foundational Models for Robotics at Tesla; Targeting World Models to Compromise Robot Learning Pipelines

Human data is the main source of scale, but it needs a bridge

EgoScale showed human-data validation loss following a log-linear scaling law that tracks real-robot success. It also found that the hardest dexterous tasks needed an aligned mid-training stage (about 4 hours of teleop paired with 50 hours of matched human data). Ego2Robot found hand-pose accuracy limits its gains on precise tasks.

See: EgoScale: Scaling Dexterous Manipulation with Diverse Egocentric Human Data; Being-H0.5: Scaling Human-Centric Robot Learning for Cross-Embodiment Generalization; RoboTTT: Context Scaling for Robot Policies; Data Infrastructure for Physical AI; Ego2Robot: Scalable Robot Data Synthesis from Egocentric Human Data

Force and touch are being added to VLAs

Several papers traced VLA failures in contact-rich tasks to missing force signals or to flow-matching noise schedules that underweight small near-contact actions. The fixes are cheap: learned torque estimates for commodity arms, tactile arrows drawn on images, and force experts added late in post-training. Wolfram Burgard (University of Technology Nuremberg) and Sharpa both argued that robot foundation models need force and touch.

See: FACTR 2: Learning External Force Sensing for Commodity Robot Arms Improves Policy Learning; PACE: Temporal-Role Tactile Conditioning for Chunk-Based Insertion Policies; Demystifying When and Why VLAs Fail in Contact-Rich Tasks and How to Fix Them; Visible Touch: Rendering Contact for Visuomotor Policies; Challenges to Realize Embodied AI

RL returns as targeted post-training

RL was mostly applied on top of a pretrained generalist, not as a replacement for one. RL Token runs a small actor-critic on compressed VLA embeddings to fix a task's hardest step within hours; its Ethernet-insertion episodes were faster than an expert teleoperator's. π0.7 instead conditions on quality metadata so it can learn from mixed-quality data, and Play2Perfect shows that RL play pretraining in simulation makes sparse-reward assembly learnable.

See: RL Token: Bootstrapping Online RL with Vision-Language-Action Models; π0.7: a Steerable Generalist Robotic Foundation Model with Emergent Capabilities; Play2Perfect: What Matters in Dexterous Play Pretraining for Precise Assembly?; Learning Without Starting Over: Expanding What Robots Can Learn in the Real World

Evaluation is not trusted

A best-paper finalist showed that a DINO-plus-MLP policy nearly matches the best reported LIBERO scores, and that few reported gains are statistically significant. RoboColosseum estimated 400-500 comparisons are needed per significant conclusion. LIBERO-CF found VLAs often ignore language when they see objects from training tasks.

See: What Are We Actually Benchmarking in Robot Manipulation?; RoboCap & RoboColosseum; When Vision Overrides Language: Evaluating and Mitigating Counterfactual Failures in VLAs; ATOM-Bench: A Real-World Benchmark for Atomic Skills and Compositional Generalization in Manipulation Policies

Deployment numbers and the revenue question

Industry talks reported operating metrics. Atlas sequencing parts at Hyundai: 98.6% completion with a 9.5% intervention rate, but cycle time is about 50% too slow to pay off. Walden's Toyota camshaft cell has never stopped the line. Gemini Robotics 2.5 reported 96% autonomous kitting. The debate split on whether paying people to collect in-domain data can ever be economical outside tasks like laundry.

See: Learning in the factories; Actions and Agents for True Long-Horizon Behavior.; Building general purpose robots with Gemini; Debate: "The current recipe of behavior cloning pre-training and in-domain fine-tuning will lead to commercially viable generalist robots"

Since CoRL 2025

Three times the papers, twice the people

Seoul accepted 263 papers, from roughly 700 submissions according to the 2026 chairs, and an unofficial count put in-person attendance at 2,441. Austin received 2,094 submissions and had about 4,800 attendees, and to handle them it added a poster-only tier, senior area chairs, a first-round rejection before rebuttal, and citation screening.

Awards moved from task results to learning machinery

In 2025 the Best Paper awards went to unified position/force control for legged loco-manipulation and to Fabrica's dual-arm assembly, and π0.5 and DexUMI were finalists. The 2026 winners were a learned world model used to train humanoid control (SuperMimic) and context scaling (RoboTTT).

Model releases are now measured in factories

Google DeepMind announced Gemini Robotics 1.5 around CoRL 2025. In Austin, Gemini Robotics 2.5 came with a production-line kitting pilot. Physical Intelligence went from π0.5 to π0.7. The 2025 special panel discussed humanoid learning; the 2026 one debated commercial viability.

From VLAs and real2sim to agents and world models

A 2025 survey named VLAs, Real2Sim2Real and tactile sensing as CoRL's main themes. All three continued in 2026, and Real2Sim2Real got its own late-breaking track. What is new is the agentic robotics track and world action models trained jointly on video and actions, which were not 2025 themes.

Looking ahead to 2027

Evaluation norms will tighten in Paris

Prediction: more papers will report confidence intervals and use held-out or post-release test sets, and reviewers will question gains on LIBERO and SimplerEnv. Evidence: a best-paper finalist on exactly this, RoboColosseum's free evaluation offer, and keynote calls for deadline and interventions-per-hour metrics.

Layer interfaces become the research object

Prediction: work will shift to what passes between System 1 and System 2: failure signals going up, force or contact intent going down. Evidence: Zhao's handoff-failure analysis, Agrawal's claim that System 1 is the bottleneck, and agentic harnesses at NVIDIA and Boston Dynamics.

World models will be judged on whether you can train in them

Prediction: world-model papers will need to show that policies trained inside them don't exploit errors, and that success predicted inside the model matches real-world success, not just that the video looks right. Evidence: Tesla's warning, MemWorld's still-underestimated success, and the poisoning attack.

Throughput replaces success rate in deployment claims

Prediction: 2027 industry talks will lead with cycle time, mean time between interventions and number of robots per remote operator. Boston Dynamics forecast Atlas's first 8-hour shift in 2027, and Goldberg framed the year's challenge as revenue. Whether BC pretraining plus in-domain data clears that bar will remain contested.

Key takeaways

  1. The field has largely converged on a stack; the open question is economics, not architecture.
  2. Frontier models help as planners but failed as direct step-by-step controllers under a real clock.
  3. Human egocentric data scales, but precise dexterity still needed aligned robot-and-human mid-training.
  4. Force and touch can be added to existing VLAs cheaply, and doing so fixes specific contact failures.
  5. Most RL now targets a pretrained generalist's bottleneck step instead of training from scratch.
  6. Popular simulation benchmarks can be largely solved by memorization, and most reported gains are within noise.
  7. Best Paper went to training humanoid control inside a learned differentiable world model.
  8. Deployments reach about 99% completion with interventions but remain too slow to pay off.

Semantic Anchoring for Robotic Action Representations

Yuan Xu · Peking University (presenter not named; first author shown)

All authors

Yuan Xu1, Youheng Shi1, Chengyang Li2,3, Wentao Zhu2, Yizhou Wang1

1Peking University2Eastern Institute of Technology, Ningbo3Shanghai Jiao Tong University

Inspired by mirror neurons, the authors show that VLA action representations lose their alignment with pretrained vision-language semantics during fine-tuning, and that this alignment correlates with task success. They split features into a shared semantic path aligned with a frozen VLM and a private path that keeps execution details, improving several VLAs (including pi0) in simulation and on real robots.

Key takeaways
  • Semantic alignment of action representations measurably erodes during fine-tuning and tracks both rollout success and out-of-distribution generalization.
  • Aligning all features to semantics hurts execution, so a shared/private feature split is needed to preserve physical details.
  • Gains are larger on generalization axes such as compositional tasks than on in-domain tasks.

MEM: Multi-Scale Embodied Memory for Vision Language Action Models

Marcel Torne · Physical Intelligence / Stanford University

All authors

Marcel Torne1,2, Karl Pertsch1, Homer Walke3, Kyle Vedder1, Suraj Nair1, brian ichter1, Allen Z. Ren1, Haohuan Wang1, Jiaming Tang1,4, Kyle Stachowicz1,3, Karan Dhabalia1, Michael Robert Equi1, Quan Vuong1, Jost Tobias Springenberg1, Sergey Levine1, Chelsea Finn1, Danny Driess1

1Physical Intelligence2Stanford University3UC Berkeley4MIT

The authors add multi-scale memory to VLAs: short-term visual memory of the last few seconds compressed by a video encoder and fed to the low-level policy, plus long-term textual memory in which the VLM recurrently predicts a summary of past events. This lets policies handle tasks lasting up to 15 minutes, such as preparing a recipe and cleaning the kitchen.

Key takeaways
  • Naively feeding raw video history is infeasible: one hour at 50 Hz with 256 tokens per image is about 50 million tokens.
  • Ablations show both long-term text memory and short-term video memory are needed to solve the long-horizon tasks.
  • Memory enables in-context adaptation, where the policy notices a failed attempt and tries a different strategy instead of repeating the same mistake.

PRTS: A Primitive Reasoning and Tasking System via Contrastive Representations

Yang Zhang · TeleAI, China Telecom / Tsinghua University (presenter not named; first author shown)

All authors

Yang Zhang1,2, Jiangyuan Zhao1,3, Chenyou Fan1,4, Fangzheng YAN1, Tian Li1, Haitong Tang1, Sen Fu1, Xuaner Wu1, Qizhen Weng1, Weinan Zhang3, Xiu Li2, Chi Zhang1, Chenjia Bai1, Xuelong Li1

1TeleAI, China Telecom2Tsinghua University3Shanghai Jiao Tong University4Fudan University

PRTS reformulates VLA pretraining as goal-conditioned contrastive RL, treating language instructions as goals so that state-action embeddings capture progress toward the goal without reward annotation, combined with action-token prediction and scaled to 400 million samples. The resulting policy recovers from heavy human interference, follows novel instructions zero-shot and handles unseen situations in long-horizon tray clearing.

Key takeaways
  • Same-instruction steps are treated as positives and later steps are weighted more, so the embedding encodes temporal progress toward the goal.
  • The contrastive pretraining needs no extra reward labels, and downstream post-training uses standard behavior cloning with a fresh flow-matching action expert.
  • The policy handled an empty-bowl case absent from all demonstrations, suggesting progress-aware representations aid recovery.

Learning What to Say to Your VLA: Mostly Harmless Vision Language Action Model Steering

Hyun Joe Jeong · Carnegie Mellon University

All authors

Hyun Joe Jeong1, Gokul Swamy1, Andrea Bajcsy1

1Carnegie Mellon University

The authors learn a language feedback policy that steers a frozen VLA by searching locally perturbed language sequences, rolling them out closed loop, and distilling the best ones into a VLM via rejection fine-tuning. An improvement head with conformal prediction decides when to intervene, giving a do-no-harm guarantee, and success rises on pi0.5 and MolmoAct2 in simulation and on hardware.

Key takeaways
  • VLA language-to-action mappings are brittle, so rephrasing an instruction can change behavior unpredictably.
  • Conformal prediction on the improvement head lets the system fall back to the original instruction when steering is unlikely to help.
  • Steering through language alone improves VLA performance without updating the underlying policy weights.

When Vision Overrides Language: Evaluating and Mitigating Counterfactual Failures in VLAs

Yu Fang · University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill

All authors

1University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill

The authors introduce LIBERO-CF, a benchmark of over 50 counterfactual tasks showing that VLAs often ignore language and repeat well-learned training tasks because of vision shortcuts. They propose Counterfactual Action Guidance, a dual-branch inference scheme that amplifies the difference between language-conditioned and unconditioned action predictions, improving language grounding in simulation and with pi0.5 on real robots.

  • VLAs on LIBERO reach high success even with vision-only input, revealing strong reliance on visual cues.
  • Counterfactual failures are triggered by the presence of training-task objects; removing them helps the policy follow the instruction.
  • CAG needs no extra demonstrations or changes to the base VLA architecture or weights.

EmbodiSteer: Steering Embodiment-Agnostic Visuomotor Policies with Joint-Space Guidance for Zero-Shot Cross-Embodiment Deployment

Shihefeng Wang · Tsinghua University

All authors

Shihefeng Wang1, Kangchen Lv1, Mingrui Yu1, Xiang Li1

1Tsinghua University

EmbodiSteer keeps a Cartesian visuomotor policy, trained from handheld-gripper data, frozen and moves diffusion sampling into the target robot's joint space via forward kinematics and the Jacobian. An optimization-based guidance at each denoising step balances collision clearance against changes to end-effector motion, enabling zero-shot, body-aware deployment across nine simulated and two real robots.

Key takeaways
  • Denoising in joint space leaves task performance almost unchanged while adding whole-body awareness.
  • Naive gradient-based collision guidance pulls the gripper off the task, so the constraint must be balanced against end-effector motion.
  • On real tasks, success nearly doubled and collisions fell from 97% to 7% without robot-specific training.

Diagnosing Compositional Generalization in Sequential Robot Tasks

Yixiao Wang · UC Berkeley

All authors

Yixiao Wang1, Cheng-En Wu1, Lingfeng Sun1, Pengcheng Wang1, Xiang Ji1, Boyuan Liang1, Guojian Zhan1, Masayoshi Tomizuka1

1UC Berkeley

The authors decompose the compositional generalization gap in sequential tasks into marginal instruction shift, instruction-compositional shift and context-action shift, and use it to ask which instruction combinations a robot actually needs to see. Experiments show sparse but well-chosen coverage suffices, and that failures under sparse coverage stem from instruction steering rather than missing skills.

Key takeaways
  • Full combinatorial coverage is unnecessary; a carefully chosen quarter of the data can match full training.
  • Adding a single demonstration per task fixes many sparse-coverage failures, pointing to instruction steering as the bottleneck.
  • When instructions depend on each other, relational coverage of dependent pairs is required and modular policies with oracle high-level indicators fail.

Early-Career Keynote

Organizing Physical AI Across Timescales

Hang Zhao · Tsinghua University (IIIS) / Galaxea AI

Introduced by Chen Feng, Hang Zhao argues that robot intelligence should be split by timescale rather than function: S2 reasoning over seconds, S1 policies at tens of hertz and S0 control at hundreds of hertz. In a RoboCasa study with a real clock, frontier models used as direct or agentic step-by-step policies collapsed to 15-20% success, while orchestrated designs that pair a slow model with fast learned skills held up. He draws on lessons from DriveVLM's dual system in production cars and from robot parkour, then presents three ways to keep S1 fast: FastWAM, which learns from video at training time but skips video generation at test time (G0-7); turning off chain-of-thought at inference in G0.5; and the streaming action tokenizer ActionCodec 2. He closes by arguing that interfaces between layers need richer feedback going up and force or contact intent going down, and he asks benchmarks to report success under a deadline along with inference time.

Key takeaways
  • Slow frontier models are fine when combined with a fast lower layer, but used as step-by-step direct policies under a real clock they fail most tasks.
  • Layer boundaries should be drawn where the required reaction time changes: task and scene at seconds, hand-object motion at tens of hertz, contact and force at hundreds of hertz.
  • Skipping chain-of-thought at inference made no difference on about 90% of tasks and doubled speed, helping mainly on long multi-stage tasks.
  • Skipping future-video generation at test time sped up a world action model by more than four times, and the 16B G0-7 runs at 60 ms per action on an RTX 5090.
  • Most failures occur at handoffs between layers, because lower layers cannot report failure upward and motion-only commands omit force.
  • Zhao suggests S2 belongs in the cloud, S1 on edge servers shared by multiple robots, and S0 on the robot itself.

Physical Intelligence presents π0.7, a generalist robot foundation model trained with rich multimodal prompts: subgoal images, detailed subtask instructions, and metadata describing how a task was performed (e.g., quality, mistakes, speed). Out of the box, a single model matches or sometimes exceeds the task-specific RL-post-trained π*0.6 on laundry folding, espresso making and box folding, and also performs tasks from earlier PI releases without fine-tuning. Key takeaways

  • Conditioning on quality metadata lets the model separate good from bad behaviors so high-quality behavior can simply be requested at test time, similar to telling an LLM to act as an expert.
  • π0.7 transferred laundry folding to a UR5 bimanual industrial arm despite large embodiment differences from the lightweight arms used for data collection.
  • Determining data quality remains an open, iterative problem; PI relies on post-hoc annotations refined by what correlates with real-world performance.

Key takeaways
  • Finn argues that reliability is the key to commercial value and that it comes from large in-domain data plus post-training optimized for success, which is how LLMs became useful.
  • Kaelbling argues that BC has not shown 'explosive generalization' because physical tasks, unlike language, were not designed to be learnable, and that learned models are more disentangleable than policies.
  • Johns argues that paying people to collect task-specific data is uneconomical for most tasks (laundry folding is the exception) and points to simulation as a likely route.
  • Agrawal argues that system-2 planning is largely solved by LLMs, the bottleneck is system-1 contact-rich control, and the right metric is throughput (speed times reliability), not accuracy.
  • Toussaint argues that narrow commercial domains can be covered by data even if this is not the best science, while Bisk argues that imitation alone cannot produce the reasoning about the unseen that safety-critical deployment needs.
  • Much of the debate turned on definitions, such as whether simulation and RL fine-tuning count as part of 'the current recipe'.
